There's a reason we work one student at a time. It isn't an upsell.

A teacher with twenty students in a classroom is doing triage. What got covered? Which kids raised their hand? Did anyone look completely lost? The next week's plan gets written from those signals, which are mostly absence. You rarely see the student who understood the reading but couldn't find the words to say so. You almost never see the student who faked understanding to avoid being called on.

One-on-one collapses that distance. When there's only one student in the session, you can hold what they're thinking about, what they half-understand, what they care enough to argue over. You can stay on a page for forty minutes if the page deserves forty minutes. You can follow a tangent that turned out to be the actual lesson.

## The Kanban board

Every student has a shared board. Parents see it, students see it, the teacher updates it every session.

Three columns: goals for the quarter, what we worked on this week, what we noticed. Not grades. Not scores. Notice, meaning: "started writing topic sentences that commit to a position" or "needs a new strategy for the reading comprehension section on timed tests."

Visible progress is the antidote to term-end surprise. It's also how students come to see themselves as learners who are actually getting somewhere, rather than as test-takers who occasionally score well.

## Why now

The AI question is not that machines will replace teachers. It's that a student who can get an answer in two seconds will lose the habit of sitting with a question long enough to understand it. Sitting with a question is where thinking happens.

The way to protect that habit is to sit there with them.
